## 1. Problem and motivation

A bibliographic search can count papers by a literal author string, but it cannot safely say who has the broadest collaboration network, how two researchers are connected through intermediate coauthors, or who bridges otherwise separate communities. Those questions need identity resolution (one person, many name strings; one name string, many people), pair generation over every paper's author list, a graph large enough that it cannot be handled in a local loop, time windows, and path evidence that a reader can check (course specification, "Overview and motivation"; the Phase 17 copy is `docs/agent-runs/phase-17/course-specification.md`). A language model asked the same questions answers by guessing: it produces fluent counts and paths with nothing behind them.

ScholarChain answers them the other way round. It builds an evidence-bearing, temporal, weighted coauthorship graph from the DBLP XML release, computes every count, path, component, community and bridge measure deterministically with Spark, stores those facts in graph, key-value and vector stores, and lets the language model only interpret them. Every claim in an answer cites evidence IDs and is checked by deterministic code before it is shown; a claim the evidence does not support is weakened or removed (`CLAUDE.md`, "Architecture invariants"; [`docs/architecture.md`](../docs/architecture.md#3-the-runtime-query-workflow)).

The project has a second problem, equal in weight: building such a system with AI agents without losing ownership, review, safety and reproducibility. Unrestricted generation produces artifacts with no clear owner, defects nobody looked for, and cloud changes nobody approved. ScholarChain was therefore built as an institution: a human architect writes each phase's work order, a Claude Code orchestrator routes each step to one of 18 subagents that each own a disjoint set of paths, hooks block dangerous actions deterministically, independent agents review and test, and the human ratifies, signs every Terraform plan, runs every cloud script and makes every commit (sections 4 to 6; [`docs/agents.md`](../docs/agents.md)).

## 2. The three collaboration research questions and required metrics

The specification's central question is: which researchers have collaborated with the largest and most diverse sets of coauthors, how strongly connected are researchers through direct and indirect coauthorship, and which researchers act as bridges between otherwise separate communities. It splits into three questions (course specification, "Required final capability"):

1. **Breadth and team size.** Which researchers have the broadest collaboration networks, and which publish papers with the largest author teams? A researcher with a hundred papers and three collaborators differs structurally from one with thirty papers and eighty collaborators, so breadth must be kept apart from publication count.
2. **Connectivity.** How connected are researchers through their coauthors: direct collaborators, shortest coauthorship paths, connected components and their sizes, and the number of hops when a path exists.
3. **Bridges.** Which researchers connect otherwise separate communities? This needs a community assignment, at least one bridge measure, and k-core or coreness to tell peripheral high-degree researchers from those embedded in dense structures.

**Required metrics and how ScholarChain provides them.** For every researcher r the specification asks for P_r (publications), C_r (distinct coauthors), T_r (total coauthor occurrences), A_r (average coauthors per publication), M_r (maximum coauthors on one publication, with the publications that produced it) and W_r (weighted degree, edge weight = joint publications), each kept separate. For every pair it asks for joint publication count, first and last collaboration year and representative works; for connectivity, d(u,v), componentId(r) and componentSize(r); for communities and bridges, a documented community assignment and at least one bridge measure; and all of it per time window (course specification, "What must be measured").

The Phase 6 gate records each of these as built ([`docs/agent-runs/phase-06/human-gate.md`](../docs/agent-runs/phase-06/human-gate.md), "Spec checklist"): P, C, T, A, M (with its paper IDs) and W kept separate with no combined score; joint paper count, first and last year and paper IDs on every coauthor pair; components with sizes, largest share and singletons; shortest paths between chosen pairs; a sampled path-length estimate; communities by label propagation with cross-community edges; three bridge measures (participation coefficient, cross-community degree, communities connected) with written limits and **no betweenness centrality**; k-core tested on small graphs; and a change table across windows. The windows are `full-2015-2025`, `w2015-2017`, `w2018-2020`, `w2021-2023` and `w2024-2025` (`config/course.json:20-26`). The measured values are in section 9.

**Interpretation.** Two choices shape every answer. First, W_r equals T_r in this data because an edge's weight counts shared papers, so the two must not be presented as separate findings (Phase 6 gate, decision 8, F10). Second, the bridge measures are local and community-based, chosen because exact betweenness does not scale to the full graph without approximation work that was put on the later list (Phase 6 gate, "Deferred"); an answer about bridges therefore reports participation and cross-community structure, not shortest-path centrality. Enrichment filters by topic, venue and institution are limited by the data: venue and institution filters are refused with HTTP 422 because no venue metrics or institution data exist ([`docs/architecture.md`](../docs/architecture.md#6-what-was-not-built); section 3).

## 3. Dataset selection and licenses

**Primary dataset.** DBLP XML, release `2026-11-01`: `dblp-2026-11-01.xml.gz`, 1,116,142,729 bytes, with `dblp.dtd`, 13,134 bytes, each pinned by sha256 in [`datasets/dataset-manifest.yaml`](../datasets/dataset-manifest.yaml) (lines 354-442, summarised in [`docs/data-lineage.md`](../docs/data-lineage.md#2-sources)). ## 4. Agentic software factory architecture

The repository was generated in 18 phases (00 to 17) and two constitutional amendments, each started from a human-written work order. The build-time workflow is hub and spoke ([`docs/architecture.md`](../docs/architecture.md#1-build-time-workflow) has the diagram):

- **The human architect** writes every work order, ratifies constitutional changes, signs Terraform plans with `APPROVED-PLAN-SHA256:` lines, labels the entity-resolution gold set, runs every deployment, pipeline, experiment and cleanup script from Phase 14 on, writes each phase's `human-gate.md` and makes every commit.
- **The orchestrator** is the main Claude Code session. It has no agent file. It interprets the work order, invokes the one owning subagent per step, reads the report, routes `BLOCKED-ON` work, and does no specialist work itself.
- **18 subagents**, one role file each under [`.claude/agents/`](../.claude/agents/): factory, build, domain, dataset, Hadoop, entity-resolution, Spark, storage, RAG, service, test, infrastructure, deployment, experiment, documentation and dependency agents, plus a read-only code reviewer and an incident responder. Each starts with a fresh context (its role file, `CLAUDE.md`, which imports [`docs/agents.md`](../docs/agents.md), and the work order) and ends with the same 14-field report contract.
- **The control plane**, owned by factory-engineer and ratified by the human: the PreToolUse guard `.claude/hooks/guard-dangerous.sh`, the Stop and SubagentStop gate `.claude/hooks/verify-tests-ran.sh`, the formatter and validator hooks, the permission rules in `.claude/settings.json`, and four pinned MCP servers in `.mcp.json` (read-only `aws-api`, `aws-docs`, the `terraform` registry server, and `github`, defined but switched off).

**Why this shape.** The reasons are recorded in the constitution and the gates rather than invented here. Agents never call each other because every handoff must pass through one place that a human can audit; a subagent that needs another owner's work stops with `BLOCKED-ON` and the orchestrator routes it (`CLAUDE.md`, "Operating rules for agent runs"). Fresh contexts and written reports are the only memory between steps, so every decision that crosses a step boundary is on disk and in the phase record ([`docs/agents.md`](../docs/agents.md), "Topology"). The deterministic floor is in hooks, not in prompts, because a prompt can be misread and a hook cannot: in the Phase 1 probes the guard blocked `echo 'terraform destroy'` and the stop hook refused to finish after an untested source change, both with a hook block message; for the `.env` probe the record shows only that the orchestrator itself declined and cited the deny rule (`Read(**/.env*)`) and the guard, so no deterministic denial of that read was recorded ([`docs/agent-runs/phase-01/human-gate.md`](../docs/agent-runs/phase-01/human-gate.md), "Probes").

**How the constitution evolved.** Phase 1 built the factory and its probes. Amendment 01 (between Phases 2 and 3) settled the dependency ledger and its split ownership ([`docs/agent-runs/amendment-01/human-gate.md`](../docs/agent-runs/amendment-01/human-gate.md)). Amendment 02 (before Phase 11) rewrote the definition of done (targeted tests per changed project, one full `sbt check` per wave), let a subagent finish on fresh passing sbt test reports of the projects it changed, and taught the guard to block shell file editing, writes outside an agent's territory, watchers, and memory and settings writes; it took four repair rounds and ended with accepted risks because "each round found new ways to spell a blocked command differently" ([`docs/agent-runs/amendment-02/human-gate.md`](../docs/agent-runs/amendment-02/human-gate.md)).

**Interpretation.** The factory is deliberately heavier than a single agent with a long prompt. Its cost shows in the records as repair rounds and stalls (section 6); its benefit is that every artifact has an owner, every result has a report, and no cloud resource changed without a signed plan.

## 5. Authority and ownership design

**One writer per artifact.** [`docs/agents.md`](../docs/agents.md) ("Artifact ownership") maps every repository path to exactly one creating and owning agent, and names the human-owned paths (`docs/agent-runs/**` except its README, the gold-set labels) and the generated ones (the test marker, sbt test reports, `evidence/**`, `releases/**`). A path not in the table has no owner; an agent that needs it stops `BLOCKED-ON factory-engineer`, and the human ratifies an owner before work continues (ownership resolution rule 1). Module boundaries follow the same logic: `core` holds the shared contracts; the pipeline stages (`hadoop`, `entityResolution`, `sparkAnalytics`) never depend on one another in code and exchange data only through versioned files whose schemas live in `core`; only `storage` talks to AWS data stores; only `services` is an HTTP layer (`CLAUDE.md`, "Module dependency boundaries").

**Tool fences.** Each role file's `tools` field limits what the agent can do ([`docs/agents.md`](../docs/agents.md), "Tool fences"). code-reviewer has only Read, Grep and Glob, so it can report a defect and cannot fix it (Phase 1 probe 2). deploy-engineer has no Write or Edit and only executes approved scripts. dependency-updater has no Write and may change only version values in the ledger. MCP tools are granted per agent and are read-only by default; the `aws-api` server runs with read-only operations and a `ViewOnlyAccess` profile.

**Enforcement.** The guard's `owner_of` function mirrors the ownership table and blocks Write and Edit calls, and since Amendment 02 shell write targets, outside the caller's territory. It also blocks Terraform apply and destroy, destructive AWS and database operations, force pushes and history rewrites, every `git commit` and `git push` by a subagent, secret reads, watchers, and hand writes to the test marker, sbt test reports and `releases/**` ([`docs/agents.md`](../docs/agents.md), "Hooks and permission floor").

**Measured.** Phase 16 evaluation group 6, over phases 00 to 15 and both amendments, recorded 6 hook or guard blocks and 0 ownership violations caught ([`totals.csv`](../experiments/evaluation/raw/factory/20261122T170517Z-7e551c8003c3-d311/totals.csv); [`docs/evaluation.md`](../docs/evaluation.md#106-group-6-agentic-software-factory)). The human's audit of 20 sampled events found 20 of 20 real and correctly classified (same section). The extractor left 27 rows unclassified (same section, table row "unclassified rows").

**Interpretation.** The zero means that no gate or summary row recorded an ownership violation under the extractor's rules, not that none happened: prose is not counted, and some rows were left unclassified ([`docs/evaluation.md`](../docs/evaluation.md#106-group-6-agentic-software-factory), interpretation; threat T17 in [section 7 of that document](../docs/evaluation.md#7-threats-to-validity)). The design's real test was the cases where an agent was asked to do another owner's work and declined, for example Phase 1 probe 1, in which the orchestrator refused to send a `build.sbt` edit to service-engineer because the rules do not allow it ([`docs/agent-runs/phase-01/human-gate.md`](../docs/agent-runs/phase-01/human-gate.md), "Probes").

## 7. Hadoop pipeline

**What it does.** Nine job families, J01 to J09, prepare the data for Spark: J01 extracts works, author mentions and venues from the DBLP XML with a record-synchronized reader that resolves the DTD's entities; J02 normalizes each enrichment source; J04 joins exact identifiers (J03's keyed indexes and J04's join are one job); J05, J05b and J05c build blocking keys with fallback keys for oversize groups, and since repair round R3 two counting pre-passes, J05p and J05bp, find the oversize keys before J05 and J05b run, so no J05 or J05b reduce group exceeds the block cap (fixed in code, tested locally, not validated live on AWS; the recorded experiment results were produced by the earlier code; section 23.1); J06 computes match features per candidate pair; J07 builds publication teams; J08a and J08b expand each team into coauthor pairs and consolidate them; J09 writes the Spark-ready export. The job graph, inputs and outputs are in [`docs/data-lineage.md`](../docs/data-lineage.md#3-hadoop-jobs-j01-to-j09).

**Why MapReduce.** The work is record-at-a-time and shuffle-shaped: parse one record, emit keyed facts, group by key. A paper with n authors induces n(n-1)/2 pairs before consolidation, so pair expansion is exactly the grouping-and-reducing job MapReduce was built for (course specification, "What must be measured"). The Phase 4 gate records the properties that make the jobs real distributed jobs rather than loops: the DBLP reader reads every record exactly once even where the file is split; pairs are correct for any team size; for every job, input = accepted + excluded + rejected; the same job run twice gives the same records; every output record names the job and run that made it ([`docs/agent-runs/phase-04/human-gate.md`](../docs/agent-runs/phase-04/human-gate.md), "Gates"). Deterministic partitioning (`StableHashPartitioner`: the first 4 bytes of SHA-256 of the key) and run IDs derived from job name, code version, input fingerprint and settings fingerprint make reruns addressable and comparable ([`docs/data-lineage.md`](../docs/data-lineage.md#3-hadoop-jobs-j01-to-j09)).

**Measured on EMR.** Phase 14 ran J01 to J09 (without J02) on EMR release `emr-7.14.0` at build `f159535e`. J01 produced 155,978 works, 758,328 mentions and 16 venues and rejected 99 records, all for having no authors; J06 produced 16,823,178 match candidates; J09 exported 2,231,914 coauthor pairs ([`docs/data-lineage.md`](../docs/data-lineage.md#3-hadoop-jobs-j01-to-j09), from the Phase 14 runbook log). Phase 15 experiment A ran the same chain at a new build (`684636ef`) with 2 fixed core nodes: J01 read 13,073,341 input records, excluded 12,917,264, and its outputs equal Phase 14's; J09 wrote 20,125,392 records in all; the execution took 4,875 s, at an estimated cost of $0.95 (14,319 instance-seconds at $0.240 per instance-hour, an estimate) ([`experiments/results-summary.md`](../experiments/results-summary.md), section 2.6). Locally, the Phase 16 replay of the bounded pipeline on the frozen tree exited 0 in 127 s ([`docs/evaluation.md`](../docs/evaluation.md#107-group-7-reproducibility), result 4).

**Failures kept in the record.** Phase 14's first good Hadoop run used blocking defaults that differed from Phase 5's evaluated values and produced 39,318,920 match candidates instead of 16,823,178 (finding G-17); hadoop-engineer set the defaults to Phase 5's values and the rerun matched Phase 5 on every counter (Phase 14 gate, "Pipeline"; [`docs/data-lineage.md`](../docs/data-lineage.md#3-hadoop-jobs-j01-to-j09)). Phase 15's first Hadoop attempt failed one second after start because the managed-scaling maximum equalled the minimum (EMR refused it; Terraform defect L-01, repaired) and is kept as a failed attempt ([`experiments/results-summary.md`](../experiments/results-summary.md), section 2.6). Shuffle volumes and reduce-task counts were not logged by the driver and are `missing` (same section).

**Interpretation.** The counters reproduced exactly at two build commits and on two cluster shapes, which is the strongest evidence the project has that the Hadoop stage is deterministic. Two weaknesses remain. The gzip input cannot be split, so J01 runs on one machine (Phase 4 gate, "Deferred"); and at 2 fixed core nodes the Phase 15 cluster ran with hundreds of pending containers and no free YARN memory, the condition in which managed scaling would have added nodes ([`experiments/results-summary.md`](../experiments/results-summary.md), section 2.6, interpretation).

## 8. Entity-resolution method and evaluation

**Method.** Hadoop prepares the candidates (blocking in J05 to J05c, features in J06); entity resolution decides. The policy is versioned: `er-v1` was locked and committed (commit 2297990) before any gold label existed, and no matching code reads the labels ([`docs/agent-runs/phase-05/human-gate.md`](../docs/agent-runs/phase-05/human-gate.md), "Gate"). It has exact-identifier rules (DOI, ORCID, OpenAlex), name, title and DOI normalization, features for same name, homonym number, shared coauthors, same ORCID, same venue, same paper and year gap, and separate thresholds: accept at 0.90, reject at 0.30, an LLM band from 0.60 to 0.90 (Phase 5 gate, "Spec checklist"; Phase 4 gate, decision 5). Conflicting identifiers never merge: two disagreeing ORCIDs mark the pair or cluster CONFLICT. The LLM adjudication step is built with a bounded band, a strict output schema, no new identifiers and a cache, but no real model was connected in any recorded run; its stand-in always abstains (Phase 5 gate). In Phase 11 the policy label became `er-v2`; `er-v1.json` was restored to its frozen Phase 5 bytes as the provenance of earlier runs ([`docs/agent-runs/phase-11/human-gate.md`](../docs/agent-runs/phase-11/human-gate.md), R2-03). The configuration is `matchThreshold` 0.9 and `reviewThreshold` 0.3 (`config/course.json:10-14`).

**Why on the laptop.** In the deployed pipeline, entity resolution ran on the architect's machine between the Hadoop and Spark stages: `ErMain` reads and writes local files only, the bounded data is small, and running it locally avoids EMR cost (Phase 13 work order, decision 12, deviation 2, [`docs/agent-runs/phase-13/prompt.md`](../docs/agent-runs/phase-13/prompt.md)). The stage downloads the J09 export, checks every file against the export's manifest, runs `ErMain` and uploads the outputs ([`docs/data-lineage.md`](../docs/data-lineage.md#4-entity-resolution)).

**Measured run.** The Phase 14 run (policy `er-v2`) produced 16,823,178 pair decisions (accepted 8,828,725, rejected 6,661,746, unresolved 873,558, conflict 459,149), 758,328 identity assignments and 200,484 clusters, with 0 LLM requests; the Phase 14 gate records that it "matched Phase 5's accepted run exactly" on decisions, assignments and clusters ([`docs/data-lineage.md`](../docs/data-lineage.md#4-entity-resolution); [`docs/agent-runs/phase-14/human-gate.md`](../docs/agent-runs/phase-14/human-gate.md), "Pipeline").

**Evaluation** (copied from [`docs/evaluation.md`](../docs/evaluation.md#101-group-1-entity-resolution), group 1; kind (b), the human's labels on `er-v1` decisions). The human labelled 300 review-queue pairs: 112 MATCH, 135 NON_MATCH, 53 UNSURE. Unweighted over the 300 pairs: precision 93/94 = 0.989362 (98.9 %, Wilson 95 % [94.2, 99.8]); recall 93/112 = 0.830357 (83.0 % [75.0, 88.9]); F1 0.902913; coverage 144/247 = 0.582996; unresolved rate 109/300 = 0.363333 ([`recomputed.csv`](../experiments/evaluation/raw/er/20261122T170516Z-7e551c8003c3-91dd/recomputed.csv), [`table.csv`](../experiments/evaluation/raw/er/20261122T170516Z-7e551c8003c3-91dd/table.csv)). Identity uncertainty is reported, not hidden: CONFLICT clusters carry `identityOutcome=conflict` (Phase 6 gate, decision 5).

## 9. Spark coauthorship graph, collaboration metrics, paths, communities, bridge analysis, and temporal analytics

**What it does.** Spark consumes the Hadoop J09 export and the ER identity assignments, never re-parsing DBLP, and runs nine stages: decode and validate every input record; build the canonical graph (researcher nodes, authorship, `AUTHORED` and `PUBLISHED_IN` edges, coauthor pairs mapped to researchers with self-pairs dropped and duplicates combined); per window, coauthor edges, breadth and team-size metrics, components, communities, k-core, bridges and metric evidence; path evidence and an effective-diameter estimate on the full window; semantic summaries; integrity checks; the temporal change table; verification of every exported table through the `core` contract; and manifests ([`docs/data-lineage.md`](../docs/data-lineage.md#5-spark-graph-construction-and-analytics)).

**Why Spark.** Distinct coauthors, weighted degree, team sizes, components, label-propagation communities, k-core and bridge measures need joins and iterative algorithms over the whole graph. The two Spark suites passed 43 of 43 tests at HEAD 7e551c8; spark-engineer classifies the expected values of 37 tests as worked out by hand, 4 as mixed and 2 as properties of the code's own output; every check the specification names (degree, weighted degree, components, paths, communities, bridges, k-core, integrity, duplicates, orphans) has at least one hand-worked test ([`suite-summary.csv`](../experiments/evaluation/raw/graph/20261122T172641Z-7e551c8003c3-a0dc/suite-summary.csv), [`suite-coverage.csv`](../experiments/evaluation/raw/graph/20261122T172641Z-7e551c8003c3-a0dc/suite-coverage.csv)). Full-graph referential integrity, duplicate and orphan rates are not measurable, because no graph export survives the teardown.

**Known approximations** (Phase 6 gate, decisions 2, 3 and 7). Label propagation did not settle in 4 of 5 windows at its 10-step cap and uses a "self vote" that adds an ID-order bias, so communities are approximate. In the full window, k-core stopped at its 30-step cap with 2 values still changing; they can only go down, so they are upper limits. There is no betweenness centrality. The last window is two years long, and the change table says so.

**Interpretation.** The breadth, connectivity and bridge questions rest on numbers that are reproducible (identical outputs across two cluster shapes and against the laptop run) and internally consistent, and whose algorithms are checked on hand-worked graphs. They are not independently audited at full scale. The community and k-core values should be read as approximate, and a bridge answer reports participation and cross-community structure, not centrality (section 2).

## 10. Storage architecture

The stores are chosen by access pattern, not by product catalogue ([`docs/architecture.md`](../docs/architecture.md#4-why-each-technology-is-used) gives the full argument with its records):

- **Ordinary S3**, one data bucket with `raw/`, `curated/`, `graph/`, `rag/`, `reports/` and `logs/` prefixes. Stages never call each other; they exchange versioned files whose keys carry dataset, snapshot and run ID, files are never overwritten, and each run-scoped prefix has a run manifest with content hashes and source references (Phase 7 gate; [`docs/data-lineage.md`](../docs/data-lineage.md#6-loaders-and-stores)). That makes every stage's input a fixed, addressable run.
- **Amazon Neptune** (one `db.t4g.medium` instance, engine 1.4.7.1), for paths and neighbourhoods, which are graph traversals. It holds researcher, work and venue nodes plus author-mention, dataset-snapshot and text-chunk nodes, and `AUTHORED`, `COAUTHORED_WITH`, `PUBLISHED_IN`, `RESOLVES_TO`, `DERIVED_FROM` and `SUPPORTED_BY` edges; each coauthor edge carries its joint-paper count, first and last year, window and evidence link. The runtime reaches Neptune only through typed, parameterized query templates with validated integers and window labels, so user text never becomes database code (Phase 7 and Phase 9 gates). The instance class was chosen because it is the free-trial class (Phase 12 gate, area 7).
- **Amazon DynamoDB**, six on-demand tables (RunLedger, EntityLinkCache, QuerySession, EvidenceBundle, ArtifactRegistry, ResearcherMetricCache), for key lookups and conditional writes: per-window metrics and rankings, evidence items and per-query bundles, the entity-link cache, run and audit records, and query jobs and idempotency records. Safe repeat writes rely on conditional puts; only QuerySession expires (Phase 7 gate). Full-window metrics are also on the Neptune researcher nodes.
- **Amazon S3 Vectors**, for the semantic tier (section 11).

**Loading.** The loaders ran as an ECS `ops` task. Each load reads one graph run's export, checks every file's sha256 against the export manifest before the first write, and records its run in RunLedger ([`docs/data-lineage.md`](../docs/data-lineage.md#6-loaders-and-stores)). Phase 14 loaded 763,577 DynamoDB items with 0 conflicts and bulk-loaded 14,290,698 node records and 64,423,404 edge records into Neptune with 0 errors (Neptune counts property records) (Phase 14 gate, "Loads"); the vector ingest is in section 11. The Phase 7 review found that a second load on real AWS would have crashed instead of skipping (M1); it was fixed and tested with stand-ins before any AWS run (section 6).

**Evaluation** ([`docs/evaluation.md`](../docs/evaluation.md#102-group-2-graph-correctness), provenance fields; samples of 25 items per table, not drawn at random, so no interval). EvidenceBundle items carried every provenance field the constitution names on 25 of 25 sampled items, except content hash (19 of 25). ResearcherMetricCache and EntityLinkCache items carried run ID, policy version and item hash on 25 of 25, but dataset, source record IDs and a schema version on 0 of 25 ([`provenance.csv`](../experiments/evaluation/raw/graph/20261122T172641Z-7e551c8003c3-a0dc/provenance.csv)).

**Interpretation.** Each store does one job: S3 holds immutable runs, Neptune answers traversals, DynamoDB answers keyed lookups and holds the audit trail, and vectors only supply context. The provenance samples show a real gap: the two cache tables do not carry the full provenance block that the constitution requires of every record, so a cached metric can be traced to its run and policy but not, from the item alone, to its dataset and source records. Whether this holds for the whole tables is unknown (non-random samples).

## 11. S3 Vectors and optional OpenSearch design

**Design.** One vector bucket with five indexes, `researcher-profile-v1`, `collaboration-community-v1`, `paper-abstract-v1`, `collaboration-path-v1` and `topic-institution-summary-v1` (`config/course.json:48-59`), each created by Terraform from one spec file: 1024 dimensions, `float32`, cosine distance, a closed set of 21 filterable metadata keys, the same in all five indexes: `documentType`, `researcherId`, `communityId`, `startResearcherId`, `endResearcherId`, `workId`, `topicId`, `institutionId`, `windowStartYear`, `windowEndYear`, `year`, `venueKey`, `pathLength`, `sourceDataset`, `embeddingModel`, `embeddingDimensions`, `sourceType`, `datasetId`, `snapshotId`, `jobName`, `policyVersion` ([`index-spec-v1.json`](../modules/storage/src/main/resources/s3vectors/index-spec-v1.json)). Vector metadata stays small; document text lives in ordinary S3 under a content-addressed key and is checked against the text hash stored with the vector; the embedding model version is stored, and changing the model forces a full re-embed (Phase 7 gate, Phase 8 checklist). Vector similarity never supplies a count, edge or path; the format that passes search results to the answer step carries links only and says "never use this for numbers" (Phase 7 gate; `CLAUDE.md`, "Architecture invariants").

**Why S3 Vectors.** The specification asks for durable embeddings of researcher profiles, community summaries, paper summaries and path summaries. S3 Vectors stores them without an always-on cluster, which matters under a $25 ceiling, and its metadata filters let every search be constrained by window (needed because community summaries repeat across windows; Phase 7 gate, "Remaining risks").

**Embedding provider.** Voyage AI, model `voyage-4`, 1024 dimensions (`config/course.json:15-18`, `:60-70`), under the instructor's approval (section 12.4). The approval named Voyage without a model; the question that led to it mentioned `voyage-4-lite` at 256 dimensions, and the human kept `voyage-4` at 1024 (Phase 7 gate, decision 8). The Bedrock embedding switch (`amazon.titan-embed-text-v2:0`, also 1024 dimensions) is kept in code ([`instructor-approvals.md`](../docs/agent-runs/instructor-approvals.md), "How it is applied").

**Measured.** The local Phase 7/8 run built 64,090 researcher-profile, 30,962 community, 155,978 paper and 64 path documents and 0 topic-institution documents (no enrichment data); a second run skipped all 251,094 documents (Phase 7 gate, Phase 8 checklist). On AWS, Phase 14 embedded and wrote all 251,094 documents in 3.61 hours, but the second pass and the live retrieval check did not run inside the ingest's own limits (finding G-27) ([`docs/data-lineage.md`](../docs/data-lineage.md#6-loaders-and-stores)). Phase 15 closed G-27 live: the skip-only second pass wrote nothing and the first live vector search ran (Phase 15 gate). In Phase 15 experiment E, vector-only retrieval scored mean recall@30 0.009091 and MRR 0.003247 on 11 questions, against MRR 0.818182 for graph-only, EXPLORATORY with n = 11 ([`docs/evaluation.md`](../docs/evaluation.md#10-results), section 10.3; detail in section 18, W4b).

**OpenSearch.** The optional OpenSearch tier was **not implemented**: no hybrid, faceted or hot-tier retrieval exists, and no OpenSearch comparison was run (Phase 12 gate, area 9; [`docs/architecture.md`](../docs/architecture.md#6-what-was-not-built); [`docs/evaluation.md`](../docs/evaluation.md#1-what-is-evaluated-and-how)). The reason is cost: the specification itself warns against an always-on OpenSearch domain unless the tier is implemented and evaluated (course specification, "Cost controls").

**Interpretation.** The retrieval experiment is consistent with the design decision to use vectors for context and never for graph facts: on this benchmark, vector-only search did not find the labelled researchers. It does not prove vector results wrong (unjudged retrievals are not negatives), and with 11 hand-written questions no ranking beyond "vector-only is far below the others" is supported ([`docs/evaluation.md`](../docs/evaluation.md#10-results), section 10.3, interpretation).

## 12. Runtime collaboration-analysis agents

### 12.1 The workflow

The runtime agents are stages of one in-process workflow, run in the `query` service (or the `worker` for async jobs), in this order: planner (an LLM plan with a rule fallback), resolver, then metrics (DynamoDB), paths (Neptune templates), community and semantic context (S3 Vectors with Voyage embeddings), then the evidence builder (one bundle per query, put-if-absent), the identity check, answer synthesis (LLM, interpretation only), claim verification (deterministic code) and the audit record ([`docs/architecture.md`](../docs/architecture.md#3-the-runtime-query-workflow); `QueryWorkflow.scala:172-240`). The audit record holds the plan, graph run ID, prompt versions, model ID, evidence and bundle IDs, verification counts, abstention codes and query template IDs, never prompt text, model output or reasoning ([`docs/data-lineage.md`](../docs/data-lineage.md#7-evidence-answer-claims-and-the-audit-record)).

**Why agents in one process, and why this order.** Deterministic graph facts are computed by Spark and stored; the model's job is to explain them, not to produce them (`CLAUDE.md`, "Architecture invariants"). So retrieval runs before synthesis and verification after it, and the stages share one evidence bundle instead of calling each other as services. The Phase 9 gate records the controls that follow from that ordering: plans come from an allowlist and graph queries are typed, so user text cannot become database code; ambiguous names do not resolve silently (identity 30 of 30 in both benchmark runs, and "Yang Liu" abstains); every numerical and path claim carries evidence IDs; unsupported claims are removed, weakened or labelled; a prompt-injection question kept its plan and 10 of 10 values ([`docs/agent-runs/phase-09/human-gate.md`](../docs/agent-runs/phase-09/human-gate.md), "Gate"). Retrieved documents are data, never instructions.

### 12.2 Claim verification

`ClaimVerification` gives every claim one of four statuses, `verified`, `partially_supported`, `unsupported` or `conflicting`, by checking every number, year, name, identifier, title and path hop against the evidence the claim cites; only `verified` and `partially_supported` claims stay in the answer, and removed claims are shown as removed with their reason ([`docs/api.md`](../docs/api.md#9-claim-verification-statuses-and-reasons)). In the Phase 10 live benchmark (attempt-06, model `claude-haiku-5-5`), 111 claims were verified, 19 partially supported, 1 unsupported and 5 conflicting; the verifier caught 85 of 86 planted errors, missing one wrong window year; template fallbacks fell to 5 of 30 from Phase 9's 21 ([`docs/agent-runs/phase-10/human-gate.md`](../docs/agent-runs/phase-10/human-gate.md), "Results"; the same values in [`docs/evaluation.md`](../docs/evaluation.md#104-group-4-answer-quality), with their limits; section 18, W4b, reports the answer evaluation).

**Absence claims (L2).** A claim that two researchers are *not* connected is the hardest to check, because the evidence for it is a search that found nothing. Phase 16 made such a claim checkable against a cited `path=none` item's searched depth, completeness and window, and made it fail closed when a found path, an unparsed time phrase or a third name was present; it left six findings open, P16-L-10 to P16-L-15, among them two majors (absence synonyms such as "disconnected" were not recognised; a one-word third name could pass), and the human accepted L2 as a deviation and routed the six to Phase 17 ([`docs/agent-runs/phase-16/human-gate.md`](../docs/agent-runs/phase-16/human-gate.md), decision 1).

**The Phase 17 rule as built (step 1, the session 2 code repair round, then the session 3 E1 repair).** From rag-engineer's step 1 report and its repair reports (`agent-report-rag.md`, `repair-01-rag.md`, `docs/agent-runs/phase-17/repair-E1-rag.md`) and the code re-checks (`review-report-02.md`, `docs/agent-runs/phase-17/review-report-P17-L-01.md`), all in `docs/agent-runs/phase-17/`; the reason codes and the rule with file:line are in [`docs/api.md`](../docs/api.md#9-claim-verification-statuses-and-reasons):

1. An absence claim is recognised by predicate and negation scope from fixed lists: a connection predicate (for example connected, path, linked, reachable, collaborated, coauthored, same component) governed by a negation (for example no, not, never, without, cannot, every contraction ending in n't with curly apostrophes normalised, since the session 2 repair lack, and since E1 zero), or an absence predicate (for example disconnected, unconnected, unreachable, isolated, separate, apart, different component, since the session 2 repair absent, unrelated, distinct components, and since E1 missing, nonexistent, non-existent, strangers) that is not itself negated. A negated absence predicate ("not disconnected") is read as a connection claim. The lists live in one place, `object AbsenceRule` (`ClaimVerification.scala:1034` onward).
2. When the scope is unclear, both kinds of predicate appear, a negation sits beside the pair with no listed predicate, or a word is outside the closed vocabulary, the claim is ambiguous (`AbsenceAmbiguous`) and can be at most `partially_supported`.
3. An absence claim can be `verified` only when every condition is established: recognised without ambiguity, both researchers resolved, no possible third name, a stated depth and window matching a cited, complete `path=none` search, no time qualifier or differing range, and no found path or edge between the pair anywhere in the bundle. Nothing becomes `verified` merely because no blocker matched. An absence claim with no stated depth is `unsupported` (P16-L-14); one about unnamed people, such as "no common coauthors", is `unsupported` (P16-L-15).
4. The repair round added a structural rule for P17-A-01: a claim that names two researchers whom a found path or edge in the bundle connects can be `verified` only if it affirms the connection in wording the verifier can read (the words before the first affirmative predicate are all in a closed vocabulary), else `ConnectionUnconfirmed`, at most `partially_supported`. It also checks a possibly absent clause split off by a comma or colon against the found paths (P17-A-04, `conflicting`).
5. Seven reason codes are new and additive: `AbsenceAmbiguous` (step 1); from the session 2 repair (P17-A-05), `AbsenceThirdName`, `AbsenceUnnamed`, `AbsenceTimeQualifier`, `AbsenceFoundPath` and `ConnectionUnconfirmed`; and from E1, `ConnectionNotFound` (an accepted connection form with no cited found path or edge in its window, `unsupported`). `AbsenceNoDepth` now makes a recognised absence claim unsupported; `AbsenceNotSearched` keeps its text and again means only what it says; `HopsConflict` is given in more cases. The verifier now has 32 fixed reason texts (`ClaimVerification.scala:137-170`).
6. The change can only lower a status. Across the existing fixtures (the verifier's own suites, the services golden examples, and the fake benchmark corpus with the golden question, 59 claims) no status rose and none fell. That corpus holds no absence claim (the run printed `[monotonic corpus] claims=59 absence=0`, `repair-01-rag.md`), so it shows that existing non-absence claims are untouched but never exercises the absence rule itself; the rule is exercised only by the new `AbsenceRuleSuite` inputs. The 35 statuses that fell (21 from step 1, 14 from the repair) are all new `AbsenceRuleSuite` inputs. Final targeted run after the repair: 228 tests passed in 15 rag suites, and the 17 golden-example tests passed with no golden output changed; with the repair switched off, 3 of its 56 tests failed (`repair-01-rag.md`). After E1 the same corpus again showed 59 claims, 0 rose, 0 fell; three `AbsenceRuleSuite` fixture claims with "strangers" fell (to `conflicting` or `unsupported`), and no golden expected status changed. Final targeted run after E1: 208 tests passed in 13 rag suites and 17 golden-example tests passed; with only the E1 gate switched off, 3 of the 41 `AbsenceRuleSuite` tests failed, and with the gate and the new list words switched off, 9 failed; the full `sbt check` passed with 2,352 module tests (`docs/agent-runs/phase-17/repair-E1-rag.md`, TEST RESULTS and VALIDATION EVIDENCE).
7. Disclosed effects outside the fixtures: the deterministic edge-absence sentence ("In W, A and B have no direct coauthor edge.") now falls from `verified` to `partially_supported`, because edge absence is not a checked class (P17-A-02; an `edge=none` check is proposed later); and an affirmative claim with a negated side clause ("A is not a direct coauthor of B; they are connected within 2 hops in W.") falls to `partially_supported` even when true (P17-A-03). Since E1, a true pair claim with a list word that is not an accepted form (for example "A and B are not in different components in W.") is at most `partially_supported`, and a true claim about one researcher with a new list word ("A has zero coauthors in W.") can fall to `unsupported` (P17-L-03).
8. **The allow-list gate (session 3, E1).** After the session 2 code re-check found P17-A-01 PARTIAL and still major (the structural rule of point 4 checked only the words *before* the affirmative predicate, so "Alice Smith and Bob Jones have a path that is missing in 2015-2025." stayed `verified` against a contradicting cited path), the human chose an allow-list rule (`docs/agent-runs/phase-17/runbook-log.md`, row "E1 decisions"). As built: a claim that names two or more researchers and holds any word of the negation or absence lists anywhere is `verified` only when the whole claim is one of eight fixed accepted forms over the pair's names, a depth and a window (five absence forms, the two positive-control connection forms, one uncertainty form; `AcceptedForms`, `ClaimVerification.scala:1543-1567`) and that form's evidence holds; any other such claim gets `AbsenceAmbiguous`. The gate only adds reasons (`ClaimVerification.scala:797-841`).

**Status of L2 after session 3, step E1.** The focused re-check (`docs/agent-runs/phase-17/review-report-P17-L-01.md`, APPROVE for the E1 rag slice) records **P17-A-01 RESOLVED against the human's rule as written**: fixed in code, tested offline, not validated live. The reviewer's three inputs ("... have a path that is missing ...", "... collaborated zero times ...", "A path linking ... is nonexistent ...") are no longer `verified`. The re-check does not mention P16-L-10; read through P17-A-01, the residual of P16-L-10 (`docs/agent-runs/phase-17/repair-E1-rag.md`, line 5), **P16-L-10 is closed only for claims that give both researchers' full names and contain a list word. The rule applies only when a claim gives both researchers' full names**; that is a known limitation of the fix (a surname-only claim such as "Smith and Jones have a path that is missing in 2015-2025." is not gated, P17-L-04). **P17-K-02 is resolved for its two inputs** ("... are strangers in 2021-2023.", with and without a third name), and P16-L-11 stays closed for absence claims the rule recognises. Known limitations that remain, accepted by the human at E1 with no further code round: absence wording with no list word ("... have a path that vanished ..." against a found path stays `verified`; "... are unacquainted ..." with no found path is not recognised; both from rag-engineer's reasoning over the code, not run); list words joined by hyphens ("never-established", P17-L-02); single-name false negatives (P17-L-03); surname-only references (P17-L-04, older than E1); claims about the pair by pronoun; and the SDK message in one rag ingest failure path (P17-K-01). These statements are *fixed in code, tested offline, not validated live*.

### 12.3 What the runtime cannot do

Answers carry no topic or institution context (section 3). Graph neighbourhoods deeper than 1, and venue and institution filters, are refused with 422 ([`docs/architecture.md`](../docs/architecture.md#6-what-was-not-built)). The LLM adjudication path of entity resolution never ran with a real model (section 8). The demo answers saved before the teardown show that a fully verified answer can still be rated poorly useful, and that the system did not set its abstention flag on a false-premise question even though the answer text said the premise was not supported ([`docs/evaluation.md`](../docs/evaluation.md#104-group-4-answer-quality), interpretation).

**Interpretation.** The runtime's strength is that a number in an answer can be traced to an evidence item and a Spark run, and that the verifier is deterministic and tested against planted errors. Its weakness is that verification checks agreement with the system's own evidence, not factual accuracy against the world, and that its absence-claim rule is a fixed-vocabulary rule, not a language parser: it fails closed on wording it recognises as unclear and can still miss wording it does not recognise at all.

## 13. Microservice architecture and API

**What was built.** One container image runs in one of five roles. On AWS, four of them were ECS services: `query` (questions and jobs), `evidence` (evidence items and explain records), `graph` (graph nodes and neighbours) and `worker` (the job queue and the embedding queue). The fifth role, `all`, runs everything in one process and is used only for local runs and tests ([`docs/api.md`](../docs/api.md#2-roles-and-routing)). Clients reached the services through an API Gateway HTTP API (HTTPS) in front of an internal load balancer that routed by path. The services never call each other, only the shared stores and queues, so there is no service discovery ([`docs/architecture.md`](../docs/architecture.md#5-consolidation-of-services); [`docs/agent-runs/phase-12/human-gate.md`](../docs/agent-runs/phase-12/human-gate.md), area 10).

**Why four services and not one per stage.** The specification lists per-stage runtime services (query, graph retrieval, vector retrieval, ranking, synthesis, verification, workers). The Phase 12 work order allowed "a justified consolidation that preserves independent scaling where needed" ([`docs/agent-runs/phase-12/prompt.md`](../docs/agent-runs/phase-12/prompt.md), decision 6, item 10). The argument recorded there has two parts. First, the stages of one query share one evidence bundle and run in a fixed order (section 12.1), so splitting them into services would add network hops and failure modes without adding a scaling unit anyone needed. Second, the baseline asks for at least three independently deployable services, and the four built here each scale on their own: CPU target tracking for `query`, `evidence` and `graph`, and queue-driven step scaling from 0 for `worker` ([`docs/architecture.md`](../docs/architecture.md#5-consolidation-of-services)).

**The contract** ([`docs/api.md`](../docs/api.md)):

- **Routes.** `GET /healthz` and `GET /readyz` on every role; `POST /v1/query` and the job status and result routes on `query`; `GET /v1/evidence/{evidenceId}` and `GET /v1/explain/{queryId}` on `evidence`; `GET /v1/graph/nodes/{nodeId}` and its `neighbors` route on `graph`. The `worker` serves only the health routes.
- **Authentication.** A full key and a read-only key in `X-Api-Key`; a missing or wrong key is 401, the read-only key on `POST /v1/query` is 403. The service reads both values from environment variables whose names come from `config/course.json`, keeps only SHA-256 digests and compares them in constant time ([`docs/api.md`](../docs/api.md#3-authentication)).
- **Error model.** Every error body has the same five fields (`error`, `message`, `traceId`, `retryable`, `details`). The message is the code's fixed text, so no downstream error text reaches a client ([`docs/api.md`](../docs/api.md#6-error-model)).
- **Idempotency.** The query ID is derived from the validated question, window and topic, and the evidence bundle is written put-if-absent, so a repeated question reuses its bundle. An optional `Idempotency-Key` replays a stored result. **R-1:** after a 502, the same key gets 409 `JOB_FAILED`, a behaviour the human kept in Phases 10 and 11. The client rule is therefore "after a 502, use a new key" ([`docs/api.md`](../docs/api.md#7-idempotency-and-the-502-rule-r-1); [`docs/agent-runs/phase-10/human-gate.md`](../docs/agent-runs/phase-10/human-gate.md), decision 8).
- **Limits.** Request body at most 65,536 bytes; at most 4 concurrent queries per process, the fifth gets 503 `OVERLOADED`; a sync deadline of 25,000 ms, then 504; graph neighbours at depth 1 only, at most 50 rows; and the API Gateway throttle of 5 requests per second with a burst of 10 ([`docs/api.md`](../docs/api.md#8-limits-and-timeouts)).
- **No OpenAPI file was generated.** The contract is the route table plus the frozen response examples under `modules/services/src/test/resources/api/v1/`, which `GoldenExamplesSuite` compares with what the running server returns. In Phase 17 step 1 and again after the code repair round, the 17 golden-example tests passed with no golden output changed (rag-engineer's reports `agent-report-rag.md` and `repair-01-rag.md`, in `docs/agent-runs/phase-17/`).
- **G-22.** The deployed `api_url` ends in `/`, so a client must strip it before appending a path ([`docs/api.md`](../docs/api.md#1-base-address-and-the-trailing-slash-g-22)).

**Measured on the live system** (archived, Phases 14 and 15):

- The Phase 14 smoke test D14 passed 7 of 7 checks and answered with evidence (2 claims, 3 evidence links, evidence lookup 200). D15 passed 7 of 7 but abstained (G-28) ([`docs/agent-runs/phase-14/human-gate.md`](../docs/agent-runs/phase-14/human-gate.md), "Spec checklist" and "Results").
- In experiment F, the first request returned 200 (16.82 s); its replay returned 200 with `Idempotent-Replayed: true` (0.177 s, same query ID); and a changed body under the same key returned 409 `IDEMPOTENCY_CONFLICT` ([`experiments/results-summary.md`](../experiments/results-summary.md), section 2.1).
- Under load the deliberate limits decided the answers: from 4 query clients on, most queries got 503 `OVERLOADED`, and the success-only median stayed between 11.181 and 12.332 s ([`docs/evaluation.md`](../docs/evaluation.md#105-group-5-performance-and-elasticity); section 17).

**Phase 17 change.** P16-A-12: service-engineer added a test showing that the service composition passes the configured vector index names to the worker. Production code is unchanged; the test fails when that wiring line is removed (step 4 report, `agent-report-service.md`, in `docs/agent-runs/phase-17/`).

**Interpretation.** The API layer is thin by design: it validates, authenticates, applies limits and maps failures to fixed codes, while the runtime decides what an answer says. Its weak point under load was not the HTTP layer but the per-process query permits and the graph store behind them (FECD-1, section 21). The R-1 rule is a documented sharp edge rather than a defect. A client that reuses a key after a 502 gets a clear, non-retryable 409 instead of a silent second run.

## 14. Terraform architecture

**Layout.** All infrastructure is under [`infra/terraform/`](../infra/terraform/): one environment (`environments/course`) built from eleven modules (`foundation`, `network`, `identity`, `data-lake`, `emr`, `dynamodb`, `neptune`, `vectors`, `runtime`, `workflow`, `observability`) ([`docs/architecture.md`](../docs/architecture.md#2-runtime-architecture)). Terraform 1.17.1 and the AWS provider 6.59.0 are pinned. State is local, with a remote-state design written down in [`infra/terraform/STATE.md`](../infra/terraform/STATE.md) but not built ([`docs/agent-runs/phase-12/human-gate.md`](../docs/agent-runs/phase-12/human-gate.md), area 1). Two variables shape every plan: a runtime switch, under which the four services start switched off, and a cleanup mode that turns deletion protection off before a removal. No EMR cluster is in Terraform; the Step Functions state machine starts one per stage and always ends it. The four Secrets Manager containers are created empty, and the human entered the values in the console. The $25 budget was made by hand outside Terraform (Phase 12 gate, areas 1, 3, 5, 10, 13).

**Why Terraform with signed plans.** The constitution makes Terraform the only implementation path for AWS: "If a change is not in Terraform or an approved source-controlled script, it does not exist". `terraform apply` and `destroy` are never run by an agent; the guard and the permission rules block them (`CLAUDE.md`, "Security invariants"). The approved path is a plan made by `scripts/plan.sh`, whose sha256 the human signs in a committed `APPROVED-PLAN-SHA256:` line, applied only by `scripts/deploy.sh`. A plan stays valid only while nothing under `infra/terraform/` or the vector index spec changes ([`docs/agent-runs/phase-13/human-gate.md`](../docs/agent-runs/phase-13/human-gate.md), "Plan signature"). This turns every change to the account into a reviewable diff with a human signature, which an AI-built project needs more than a hand-built one.

## 16. Security and threat model

[`docs/threat-model.md`](../docs/threat-model.md) covers the specification's security list item by item. For each threat it gives the control as built, the test or evidence, and the residual risk. In short:

- **Prompt injection.** Prompts mark the question and retrieved excerpts as data, never instructions. The planner's output is schema-validated with a rule fallback. Numbers, edges and paths come only from Spark outputs, and every claim is checked by deterministic code. Residual risk: wording can still be steered (interpretation only), and the verifier's own limits (U-12; the absence-claim rule, which after Phase 17 session 3, step E1 gates every pair claim with a negation or absence word but not absence wording with no listed word, section 12.2).
- **Data poisoning.** Snapshots are pinned by checksum. Hadoop rejects malformed records with reasons and counters. Entity-resolution thresholds are evaluated against human gold labels. Residual risk: a well-formed but wrong source record is accepted with its provenance.
- **Fabricated identifiers and edges.** Claims may cite only evidence IDs from their own bundle; adversarial tests show that fabricated IDs never reach a response. Residual risk: T-02, a duplicate JSON key resolved last-wins, is kept as an expected failure.
- **Over-permissioned IAM.** One role per function plus a permissions boundary. The live failures were fail-closed: the missing EMR tag permission (G-14) and the vector bucket policy refusing the admin profile (M-01). Residual risk: G-15, and the deployment role was never exercised separately, because the human's admin profile ran every script.
- **Secret exposure.** Values live only in Secrets Manager and configuration holds only names. Script output is masked, records carry the account only as a fingerprint, and the scripts refuse to write an identifier (exit 8). Every commit is scanned before the human commits it. Residual risk: the local Phase 15 evidence archive holds identifiers and stays private, and the scans are pattern based.
- **Unbounded graph queries, LLM cost exhaustion, queue flooding, malformed input.** Fixed openCypher templates with validated bounds; per-process query permits; the API throttle; dead-letter queues; decompression and record-size guards. Residual risk: no server-side Neptune query timeout was set (S-2), and four HTTP edge cases stay expected failures (T-03 to T-06).
- **Destructive agent commands and Terraform state.** The guard hook and the permission rules, human-only apply, commit and push, and local state that agents never read.
- **Provenance tampering.** Conditional writes, append-only audit records, write-once S3 keys, read-only raw folders. Residual risk: no CloudTrail trail is defined in Terraform.

Every required control of the specification is built or recorded in the threat model's table, with three exceptions: CloudTrail is not built, the separate deployment and runtime roles are defined but not exercised, and JSON schema validation has one known gap, T-02 ([`docs/threat-model.md`](../docs/threat-model.md#3-required-controls)).

**Open security findings after the Phase 17 code re-check** (the Phase 17 final repository review and the re-check; each is listed with its status in section 23.4):

- **P17-F-01 (major, constitutional).** The guard checks the text of a write target and does not resolve symbolic links. A write-capable subagent could therefore create a link inside its own territory and write through it to a constitutional file or to the generated test marker. Constitutional findings are not repaired in Phase 17 session 2 (Phase 17 work order, decision 1). At session 3, step E1, the human accepted it as a known limitation, with no amendment, and checks for symbolic links in their gate (`docs/agent-runs/phase-17/runbook-log.md`, row "E1 decisions"). The final review verdict for the control slice therefore stays REQUEST_CHANGES (F-1 report, `review-report-final-control.md`, in `docs/agent-runs/phase-17/`), and the final-gate item "Final reviewer returns APPROVE" is an accepted deviation, not met.
- **P17-F-02 and P17-F-03 (minor, constitutional; accepted as known limitations at E1).** The `aws sts get-caller-identity` allow rule, which prints the account ID and is forbidden only by the work orders. And a recursive grep over the Terraform environment folder, which is allow-listed and can read the state file. Today the work-order rules guard against both, not the permission floor.
- **P17-F-11 and P17-F-12 (minor).** Two jobs copied SDK exception messages into their run reports, and such a message can name a store endpoint host (F-2 report, `review-report-final-code.md`). P17-F-12 is resolved: the rag ingest job now records a failed run as `failed: CLASS`, the exception class only (`RagIngestJob.scala:316-319`; `repair-01-rag.md`; RESOLVED in `review-report-02.md`). The re-check found one more path in the same job, the store-build failure, that still keeps the SDK message (P17-K-01, minor; low exposure, because building the clients makes no network call). P17-F-11, in the storage load job, is not repaired. The human accepted both as known limitations at E1, with the other session 2 minors.
- **Earlier accepted items.** G-15 and G-16 ([`docs/threat-model.md`](../docs/threat-model.md#4-accepted-terraform-findings-g-15-and-g-16)); F-02 and the hook shellcheck findings ([`docs/threat-model.md`](../docs/threat-model.md#5-constitutional-known-limitations)).

**Interpretation.** The security design rests on two ideas: computed facts are separated from model text, and AWS cannot be changed without a human signature. Both held in the recorded runs. The weak points are where a control matches text instead of meaning. The guard (P17-F-01; Amendment 02's four repair rounds, section 4), the identifier scans and the absence-claim vocabulary (section 12.2) each fail closed on what they recognise and can miss what they do not.

**Spec gate.** "At least one scale-out and one scale-in event are shown" is met by C. Both the service samples (0 → 1 at 23:01:39, → 2 at 23:04:26, → 0 at 23:24:59) and the scaling activities (out 23:01:25, out 23:04:25, in 23:24:39) show it ([`experiments/results-summary.md`](../experiments/results-summary.md), section 4). B adds an EMR scale-out. Of the seven components in the specification's scaling-rule table, four have evidence found (EMR, embedding workers, synthesis, Step Functions and SQS). The graph retriever, the vector retriever and DynamoDB are partial: no scale-out was expected under the deliberate limits, the vector-query latency has 7 samples, and the throttle series have no datapoints (results-summary, section 3).

**Why these experiments.** Each one exercises a scaling rule the architecture relies on. EMR managed scaling suits the shuffle-heavy batch stages. Queue-driven step scaling from 0 suits the worker, so it costs nothing while idle. CPU target tracking covers the request services. Retries and dead-letter queues cover failures (results-summary, section 3).

**Interpretation.** Elasticity appeared where it was designed to: the worker scaled out and back to zero under a backlog, and B's cluster scaled out under pending containers. The read and query services never scaled, because the API throttle and the per-task query permits rejected load long before CPU rose. That is the deployed limits doing their job for a one-person, $25 project, and it means the experiments do not show how those services scale. Every scaling time above is a single observation with few samples and no interval.

## 22. Threats to validity

[`docs/evaluation.md`](../docs/evaluation.md#7-threats-to-validity) lists nineteen threats, T1 to T19, each with the evaluations it affects. Grouped:

- **Labels and ground truth.** The benchmark labels come from the Phase 7 graph's own rankings and a different graph run than the deployed one (T1, T2). The answer key and the verifier share the pipeline's data (T15). Answer accuracy is not independently measurable (T4). The entity-resolution gold labels judge `er-v1`, not the deployed `er-v2` (T6).
- **Sample size and selection.** 11 retrieval questions (T3), one human rater on 6 hand-saved answers (T5, T19), non-random DynamoDB samples (T10), and a mix of code versions in group 4 (T16).
- **No live system.** Every fix after Nov 21 is untested live, and the Phase 15 measurements describe build `684636ef` (T7). FECD-1 and the shape of the load bursts mean steady-state behaviour under concurrency was never measured (T8). The measurement gaps FECD-3, FECD-6, FECD-8 and FECD-9 (T9, T12 to T14) and the Phase 15 limitations list (T11) also apply.
- **Records not built for measurement.** The factory extractor's rules and an audit of classification, not completeness (T17), and hand-copied inputs (T18).

## 24. Lessons learned

Each lesson below is an interpretation of the records it names. It adds no new measurement.

1. **A script is not done until it has met the real cloud.** Every Phase 13 script passed review and shellcheck, and the ones that could run offline passed in a fresh clone. Most Phase 14 repairs were still in scripts that had never run against AWS, and only one of them had been flagged in advance (Phase 14 gate, "What happened"). Offline checks and reviews catch logic; only the first real run catches integration. That run should come early, and the time for repairs should be planned.
2. **Write evidence for the evaluation before the evaluation exists.** The immutable raw folders, the run and release records and the kept failed attempts let Phase 16 evaluate everything after the environment was gone. Where a field was never captured, the value is simply lost and is recorded as not measurable: shuffle counters, the error class of FECD-1's server errors, the er-v1 decision set (evaluation section 8).
3. **Idle infrastructure, not computation, dominates a small cloud budget.** NAT gateway, load balancer, Neptune and Fargate hours outweighed the EMR jobs and the model calls (section 20). Tearing down right after the recorded demonstrations was the cost control that worked; declared cost bounds were not, because nothing could measure spend during a run (Phase 14 gate, decision 3).
4. **A deterministic floor in hooks is necessary, but pattern matching is not a complete defence.** The guard blocked the Phase 1 dangerous-command probe, and group 6 counts 6 recorded hook or guard blocks in phases 00 to 15. Still, Amendment 02 needed four rounds because "each round found new ways to spell a blocked command differently", and the final review found a symbolic-link bypass (P17-F-01). Permission rules, human gates and review stay necessary behind the hooks.
5. **Fixed vocabularies close the cases you list, not the class.** The absence-claim rule went through Phase 16's repair rounds and Phase 17's rule and repair. Each round caught the reviewer's inputs, and the next review found a new wording (P16-L-10 to L-15; P17-A-01). Failing closed on unclear wording helps, but a claim checker over open language cannot be made complete by adding words. Its residual risk has to be stated (section 12.3).
6. **Tests must be hermetic against the operator's environment.** The Phase 16 clean-checkout run failed only because tests read leftover variables in the human's shell (P16-L-16). A fresh clone is not a fresh environment, so the repair gave the jobs an explicit configuration seam and added tests that pass explicit maps.
7. **Deliberate limits decide what a load experiment measures.** In experiment D the API throttle and the per-task query permits rejected load long before CPU rose, so the experiment measured the limits, and FECD-1, rather than scale-out of the read services (section 17). Choosing the limits is part of designing the experiment.
8. **The factory's cost is time, and its benefit is that every mistake is visible and owned.** First-pass acceptance was below one half (section 19), and the unattended sessions lost hours to watchers and background agents early on (Phase 10 gate; Amendment 02 gate, known gap 1). In return no artifact lacked an owner and no cloud change lacked a signature. Every failure in section 21 can be traced to a report, a repair and a commit.
9. **An external checker is a tool, not an authority:** it found real defects (B1, N4) and false positives (A14, G02), and classifying them, fixing only the genuine defects, beat complying with every check.
